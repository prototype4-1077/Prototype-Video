You know how to answer everybody else’s emergency but somehow miss your own call every single time it comes through. Picture your inner life as a phone ringing in the other room while you keep telling yourself you’ll grab it in a minute. Sadness calls. You send it to voicemail without listening all the way through. Exhaustion calls. You text back, later, like the body enjoys being put on hold. Loneliness calls three times in a row and you flip the screen facedown like that solved anything. Meanwhile somebody else has a flat tire, a crisis, a breakup, a bad day, and suddenly you become emotional roadside assistance. You know exactly what to say to them. Be gentle with yourself. Rest before you crash. Eat something real. Take a breath and drink some water. Funny how wise you get when your heart belongs to somebody else’s nervous system. But when your own body reaches out, you act like an underfunded customer service line in a back hallway. Long hold times. No live agent. Suspicious music. So self-love may be simpler than the internet made it sound. Maybe it is not bubble baths, luxury candles, and announcing your worth under a sunset quote. Maybe it is becoming someone your own pain can actually reach when it needs you. Answering the call before the voicemail becomes a lifestyle. Picking up when the message is still small—when the body is only tired, when the sadness is only a whisper, when the loneliness has not brought luggage. Because ignored feelings do not disappear just because you develop good manners around them. They escalate. They call back louder. They leave messages in the body. The turn is this: self-love is not selfishness. It is responsiveness, the decision to become reachable from the inside. Then come back here—to your hands, your breathing, the room you are in, and the quiet possibility that care can begin before collapse. The next time something in you hurts, could you answer yourself the way you wish somebody else would?
