Say your own name. Out loud. Now say it again — and again — thirty times, until your tongue starts tripping over it. Somewhere around the twentieth, something goes sideways. The name stops being yours. It's just a sound. A little noise your mouth makes. That's not you losing it. That's a glitch with a name. Jamais vu — the opposite of deja vu. Instead of the strange feeling familiar, the familiar goes strange. Your brain runs on a stamp. A little rubber stamp that hits every face, every word, every object, and goes: known, known, known. You never see the stamp. You only see the world it already approved. Semantic satiation is what happens when you wear that stamp out. Say a word enough times and the ink runs dry. The meaning peels off the sound like a label off a bottle. And underneath the label? The raw thing. The actual noise. The strangeness that was sitting there the whole time — just covered. You can do it with your own hand. Look at it. Really look. Past 'my hand' into the thing itself — five soft hooks, wet joints, a fold that opens and closes. Stare long enough and it becomes an object you happen to be wearing. It's a small vertigo, isn't it? The floor of the ordinary drops out. And for one second you're a stranger in your own kitchen, holding a fork you've never seen before. Here's the part I love. People spend real money chasing this feeling. The world made new, the veil lifted. And it was free the whole time — built right into the hardware. Because the familiarity was never in the thing. It was in you — a habit of prediction laid over the top so smoothly you mistook it for the world. The word didn't change. Your hand didn't change. The stamp just skipped — and for a moment you saw what's always underneath: a universe that never actually agreed to be ordinary. Wonder isn't a place you have to travel to. It's a glitch. And you can trigger it on purpose — in a chair, with a single word. So here's the test. Look at your own hand until it turns back into an object. That astonishment — it was always available. You just never stared long enough to be surprised.
