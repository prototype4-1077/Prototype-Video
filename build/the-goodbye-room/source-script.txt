He said he saw his wife during a DMT experience. She had died months earlier. He'd used changa, a DMT-containing blend. His forum account described an unexpected reunion. A dark room. His wife appeared from behind something resembling a wall, glowing. Neither spoke. Yet their shared life returned: falling in love, their children, laughter and tears. He wrote that he hadn't repeated it. He wouldn't chase what had happened. He felt he'd been able to say goodbye. Picture grief as a room inside a house you still have to inhabit. You make breakfast. Answer messages. Even laugh. Then a familiar song opens it. And everything you never finished saying is sitting exactly where you left it. Your ordinary Tuesday has become a doorway. Grief apparently never checks whether you have company. His encounter is a personal report. Our room is a metaphor. Neither establishes contact with the dead. Something in his account stays with me beyond the question of proof. Along that wall of doors, is there one you visit often, and another you avoid? Maybe relief feels like leaving the room empty. As though love needs pain standing guard. Does yours? You don't have to answer quickly. He wasn't describing a theory. He was describing his wife, and everything their life had held. Afterward, he chose to let the encounter remain something he'd experienced, without repeating it. A door opened. He didn't spend the rest of his account demanding a key. Back here, your room has ordinary sounds. Air moving. A refrigerator humming. Traffic going past. Your feet are touching something solid. Your body is breathing through this sentence. There's room here for uncertainty, too. A story can move you while its explanation stays unfinished. Perhaps you remember someone now: their laugh, their terrible directions, the particular way they said your name. Whatever you believe about where they are, those memories are meeting you here. If your unfinished goodbye had a room, what would you want to say inside it?
