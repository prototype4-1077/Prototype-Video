They hand you a menu every few years and call it power. 

Two entrees.

Red plate.

Blue plate.

Pick one.

Then everybody starts arguing across the table like the fate of civilization depends on whether the chicken came with mashed potatoes or rice.

Meanwhile, nobody is allowed in the kitchen.

You did not choose the ingredients.

You did not hire the cooks.

You did not select the suppliers.

You did not set the prices.

You did not decide who owns the building.

But because someone placed a menu in your hand, you are told the restaurant belongs to you.

That is the illusion.

Political choice is often presented as control, when sometimes it is only participation inside boundaries you never agreed to.

You are free to choose.

As long as you choose from what has already been chosen for you.

And the menu is designed carefully.

Not just to feed you.

To divide the table.

One side is told the other side wants to poison the meal.

The other side is told your side is trying to shut down the restaurant.

Now everybody is yelling, throwing napkins, and accusing strangers of destroying dinner while the owner quietly raises the prices.

And somehow, after every election, the kitchen gets bigger, the dining room gets louder, and the people eating are still asking why the portions keep shrinking.

That does not mean your vote has no value.

It means a vote is not the entire structure.

Choosing the meal is not the same as deciding how the restaurant operates.

Real power asks deeper questions.

Who writes the menu?

Who funds the kitchen?

Who owns the land beneath the building?

Who benefits when the customers hate each other more than they question the management?

Because a system can offer choice and still control the range of outcomes.

A casino lets you choose the machine.

That does not mean you own the casino.

A streaming service lets you choose the movie.

That does not mean you control what gets produced.

And politics may let you choose a representative without ever letting you touch the machinery that decides which choices are considered realistic in the first place.

Maybe that is why the arguments feel endless.

We keep fighting over what to order instead of asking why the kitchen never serves what most people actually need.

Because being handed a menu feels like freedom—

until you realize every option was written by someone who never planned to eat with you.
