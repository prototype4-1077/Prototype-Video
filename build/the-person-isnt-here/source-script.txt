You have never argued with another person. Not exactly. You argued with the version your mind assembled. I know how that sounds—like I’m excusing them and blaming your imagination. I’m not. Harm is real. Words land. Choices leave bruises you cannot meditate into confetti. The person who hurt you is not stored in your head as a complete human. Your mind keeps fragments: a tone, a slammed door, three texts, the face before “fine.” Then it opens an animation studio and fills in the missing footage. It guesses motives, predicts dialogue, adjusts the lighting, and adds a suspicious soundtrack. Psychology calls part of this theory of mind: building models of people so you can navigate them. What remains emerging is how strongly prediction shapes who you experience in real time. So when you replay the argument at two in the morning, they are not actually there. You are debating a character rendered from evidence, memory, fear, and your nervous system’s “previously on...” That is why your opponent always says the worst possible thing. Committed actor. Terrible union rules. Here is the uncomfortable turn: they are doing the same thing to you. Somewhere, a version of you lives inside someone else’s editing room, saying lines you never approved. Neither model is nonsense. Each is built from real footage. But footage is not a whole life. A camera angle can be accurate and incomplete. The character is not the person. It is the loading screen your mind built from fragments. That does not mean reopening a dangerous door or pretending harm was love. You can keep the boundary without turning your render into a prison. Maybe they were cruel. Maybe you misread one piece. Maybe both are true. Mercy is not deleting evidence. It is labeling missing footage “missing.” Look around the room. Feel your jaw. Lower your shoulders. Notice who is actually here. Picture the person you resent, then remove every sentence you invented for them. What remains—and what might change if you questioned the render without abandoning what happened?
