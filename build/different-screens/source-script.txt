Close your eyes and picture a red apple. Make it glossy. Slowly turn it beneath a kitchen light. Can you actually see it, almost like a photograph floating somewhere behind your eyelids? Or did you receive only the idea of apple—no color, no shape, no tiny grocery-store commercial? Some people see absolutely nothing, while still knowing exactly what an apple is. Established science: this spectrum includes aphantasia and hyperphantasia. Human imagination does not ship with one standard display. Apparently, consciousness arrived with different graphics settings, and reality forgot to include the instruction manual. You may have spent your entire life assuming everyone was watching the same internal screen. They are not. Some minds render in cinematic detail. Some produce a sketch. Others simply display the file name: APPLE. Established science: inner speech varies too. Some people hear a narrator commenting on nearly everything. Others think without any voice inside their head. No internal podcast reviewing every sandwich. Neither mind is empty. Neither is more conscious. They use different interfaces to organize experience. That means arguments can involve two people comparing conclusions from screens neither person can inspect. You say, “Obviously.” They say, “Obviously.” Reality waits backstage, wondering which version of obvious you ordered. This does not mean evidence stops mattering. The outside world still pushes back against our private models. It means your rendering is not humanity’s factory setting. It is one configuration among billions. And that realization can loosen something. Maybe someone who “doesn’t get it” is not refusing your picture. Maybe no picture appeared. Maybe their silence is not emptiness. Their mind may work somewhere your interface cannot display. Now come back to the room. Feel your body touching the chair. Watch light move across an object. Listen for the farthest sound. This ordinary moment is assembled uniquely inside you, yet we still find ways to meet. Later, ask someone to picture an apple. Don’t ask whether they did it correctly. Ask what actually appeared. And if their screen surprises you, what else might become worth asking?
